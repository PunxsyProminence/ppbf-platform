Floor Card

Your bay. Your bench. Everything on it, you earned — and every piece says what earned it.

●You, your coach, your guardian

The space itself is the first thing you earn. A new athlete does not open a grid of forty grey slots — that is an itemised inventory of everything they have not done, on day one. They get an empty bench, one bare hook, and a roll of wraps. The gear arrives one piece at a time; the hooks, the shelf and the nameplate arrive after that. The bay fills up as the athlete does, so it is never empty and never a shortfall.

The moment it is earned

A stamp coming down on paper, and nothing else — the same press the training card already uses. No bounce, no spin, no confetti, no fanfare sting. Kit earned over months does not deserve a slot-machine, and a gym that celebrated like one would be telling an eleven-year-old that the noise is the point.

Headgear unearned

Three channels, and any one carries it alone (Law 3): the stamp lands, the gear comes up out of its dimmed state, and a line of type says what was earned and what earned it. Under reduced motion the movement is cancelled and the words remain — which is why the sentence is the message and the animation is only the manners.

The gear bag

Everything earned, whether or not it is out on the bench. You choose what is on display; you do not choose what you have. Nothing here can be removed, expire, or be spent — that is the whole difference between this and a score.

Liners and finishes

What an athlete customises is the kit and the bay — the locker liner, the bench finish, the nameplate metal. Not the room: grounds carry meaning here, and an athlete picking a room would be picking a meaning.

Rarity is material, never colour. A gaming platform would rank these blue, purple, orange. Law 2 reserves saturated colour for safety and status alone, so a cosmetic tier may never borrow it — a gold badge outranking a green one would put a decoration in the same visual register as a medical hold. Standing is carried by what a thing is made of instead: canvas, then wood, then hide, then brass. Which is also how it works in a real gym.

Not in this screen, deliberately: no leaderboard and no other athlete's bay — the bay is visible to the athlete, their coach and their guardian, and nobody else. No comparison, no ranking, no "you are 4th". Everyone with access sees the same bay, so there is no second version of an athlete's own record. And no points balance anywhere: every piece of kit came from one discrete, permanent, named accomplishment, and says which one.

The gear artwork is placeholder geometry. Correctly sized slots with the hardware, labels and provenance lines built around them — the gloves, wraps, tin and stool themselves are for the artist, not for this file.
